The short answer

Don't guess what people want. Find proof first: people asking for it, complaining about it, or already buying something similar. Then make a better or more specific version. In this chapter you'll find 3 product ideas with proof, and pick one.

The rule: demand first, design second

Most new sellers start with "what can I print?" The ones who sell start with "what do people already want?"

A product sells when three things meet:

A specific buyer

Board gamers, cat owners, people with small flats.

A real annoyance

Something that's messy, broken, missing or doesn't fit.

A product that fits exactly

Made for their thing, their size, their name.

If one is missing, it's a hobby print, not a product.

Where to find proof

You don't need paid tools. These five places show you what people want, for free.

Marketplace search

Type your idea into Etsy or eBay. Look at what's there and what sells.

Reddit and forums

Look for "I wish", "does anyone make", "doesn't fit".

1–3 star reviews

Bad reviews on store-bought products tell you what to fix.

Facebook groups

Hobby groups are full of people asking for things.

Your own life

What annoys you at home? Others have the same problem.

Find your product in 6 steps

01

Start from your lane and buyer

Take the lane sentence from chapter 1: what + for whom. Everything below is for that one buyer.

Don't have a buyer yet? Pick a hobby or group you belong to. You'll understand their problems faster than anyone.

02

Collect 20 annoyances

Spend 30 minutes reading where your buyers talk. Write down every annoyance you find, in their words:

  • "The cards slide around in the box"
  • "My cables always fall behind the desk"
  • "Can't find a holder that fits the [model]"

Don't judge yet. Just collect 20.

03

Check marketplace demand

For each annoyance that could be solved with a print, search for it on Etsy and eBay.

What you see What it means
Nothing at all Maybe no demand. Be careful.
A few listings with reviews Good sign. Demand, room for you.
Hundreds of identical listings Crowded. Only go in with a clear twist.

Look for listings with reviews or "sold" counts. That's proof people pay.

04

Read the bad reviews

Open 3 similar products and read the 1–3 star reviews. They tell you exactly what to make better:

  • "Too small for my [thing]" → make it fit that thing
  • "Broke after a week" → make it stronger
  • "Ugly" → make it look good

Your product = the same thing, without the problem people complain about.

05

Score your top 3 ideas

Give each idea 1–3 points for each question:

Question 1 3
Proof people want it? No proof Lots of proof
Easy to design and print? Hard Easy
Prints in under 4 hours? No Yes
Easy to ship (small, light, strong)? No Yes
Can you charge $15+? No Yes

The highest score wins. A tie? Pick the one you're most excited about.

06

Make it specific

Turn the winner into one specific product. Specific sells better than general:

  • ❌ "Cable organizer"
  • ✅ "Under-desk cable tray for IKEA Bekant desks"
  • ❌ "Card holder"
  • ✅ "Card dividers for the Wingspan box"

A specific title also makes you easier to find in search.

Kevin's tip

Use brand names carefully

"Fits IKEA Bekant" tells buyers what it works with, which is usually fine. Making it look like an official IKEA product, or using their logo, is not. More in chapter 3.

The exact 20-minute research routine

This is the routine I'd follow for every new idea. Open a spreadsheet with these columns: Listing · Price · Reviews · Shop sales · Bestseller? · Est. sales.

01

Let Etsy tell you the search words

Go to Etsy, type the first word of your idea (for example "switch game") and stop. Write down every suggestion in the dropdown. Each one is something real buyers type. Do the same on Google and Amazon.

02

Open the top 10 listings

Search the best phrase. Open the first 10 listings that are not ads (ads say "Ad" or "Sponsored"). For each one, fill in your columns. The total sales of a shop are shown on its shop page.

03

Estimate sales from reviews

Only some buyers leave a review. Estimates put it at roughly 3–8% of buyers. So a quick rule:

Estimated sales ≈ reviews × 15 to 30

A listing with 40 reviews has probably sold somewhere around 600–1,200 times. It's a rough guess (it can be off by 2–3×), but it's enough to tell "nobody buys this" from "people buy this every day".

An orange Bestseller badge means the listing is selling fast right now compared to similar items. That's the strongest free demand signal on Etsy.

04

Use the free tools the pros use

  • eRank (free plan): type a keyword and see roughly how often it's searched and how many listings compete. You want searches high, competition low.
  • EverBee (Chrome extension, free plan): shows estimated monthly sales and revenue for listings while you browse Etsy.

Use them to check, not to decide. Your own eyes on the top 10 listings matter more.

05

Mine the bad reviews on Amazon too

Search the mass-market version of your product on Amazon (a plastic drawer organizer, a cable box, a game box insert). Read the 2–3 star reviews. People write exactly what's wrong: "too shallow", "doesn't fit the Alex drawer", "cards slide around". Every complaint is a product you can print.

06

Check MakerWorld downloads as a demand signal

MakerWorld and Printables show downloads and prints for every model. A free model with thousands of prints proves people want that kind of thing. You can't sell that file, but you can design your own version that's better: a different size, a version for a specific product, or personalized.

Kevin's tip

The "+1 rule"

Don't copy a winner. Take a proven product and add one thing it doesn't have: a size for a specific brand, a lid, a name, a stackable version, a set of 3. That one difference is your whole marketing message.
